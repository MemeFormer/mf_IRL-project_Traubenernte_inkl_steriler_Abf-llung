# 1. What PP28 / MCA28 and PP31.5 actually mean

There are two concepts that are often mixed together:

**The bottle finish** is the standardized geometry of the glass neck.

**The closure** is the thing fitted onto it.

For European 28 mm glass finishes, the current European dimensional standard is **EN 17829:2023**, adopted in Germany as **DIN EN 17829:2023-09**. It specifies the various 28 mm MCA screw finishes. ([dinmedia][3])

The older GME documents such as **MCA 7.5 R** have now been superseded by EN 17829, although the terminology remains ubiquitous in industry. CETIE explicitly identifies MCA 7.5 R as the returnable-glass 28 mm finish. ([cetie.org][4])

For 31.5 mm glass finishes, **DIN EN 15543:2009-09** covers screw-thread finishes for non-carbonated liquids, with current work underway to revise that standard. ([dinmedia][5])

Germany is also revising **DIN 55406**, which specifically covers 28 mm PE/PP screw caps and 28 mm aluminium roll-on closures. The DIN 2026 work programme lists the new revision as active work. ([din][6])

That is useful because it tells us something important:

**the closure/finish combination is treated as an engineered packaging system, not as “any cap of about 28 mm.”**

# 2. How an aluminium ROPP cap actually seals

A ROPP closure begins as an aluminium shell. It isn't simply a pre-threaded metal screw cap.

During industrial capping, rollers:

1. press the shell over the bottle finish,
2. form the thread directly against the glass thread,
3. form the lower tamper-evident band beneath the retaining bead,
4. compress the liner/sealing compound against the bottle mouth.

The bottle's glass thread effectively serves as the forming mandrel for the aluminium. ([ROPP.co.uk][7])

This is why your Hypothesis A needs a little refinement.

The shell is **supposed** to undergo permanent deformation during first application.

That permanent deformation is not a manufacturing defect. It is how the ROPP closure works.

The problem after opening is that:

> **You cannot reproduce the original roll-forming process simply by screwing the cap down by hand.**

A machine applied a particular combination of axial load, radial forming force, thread geometry and liner compression.

A consumer reclosure supplies none of those controlled forming forces.

Sacmi specifically describes ROPP closures as closures that are mechanically formed onto the neck, while also noting that many are nevertheless reclosable for normal consumer use. ([https://sacmi.com/][1])

Pelliconi likewise markets 28 × 15 mm ROPP aluminium closures for beverages and explicitly lists compatibility with hot filling and pasteurization **when the correct closure is specified**. ([Pelliconi Italia S.p.A.][8])

So:

### First opening does not necessarily destroy the closure.

But:

### First opening destroys the original tamper-evident configuration and eliminates the validated relationship between cap geometry, liner compression and bottle finish.

That distinction matters enormously for your application.

# 3. Hypothesis A — mechanical shell deformation

## What is true

The aluminium skirt has undergone plastic deformation.

The original thread profile was formed against the particular bottle finish.

After opening, it is no longer an unformed shell.

The tamper bridges/band have also been irreversibly altered. Depending on design, the band either remains on the bottle or remains attached to the closure after first opening. ([cetie.org][9])

The important consequence isn't that aluminium suddenly becomes incapable of carrying load.

It's this:

### The second closure is no longer an equivalent mechanical system.

The cap may feel tight. It may not leak when inverted. It may even hold a vacuum.

But the amount of **residual sealing force** can be considerably less predictable.

Packaging-engineering literature treats application torque, relaxation, closure geometry, temperature and time as coupled variables. Closure torque decreases as the components relax after application; elevated temperature can accelerate that process. ([Packaging World][10])

So I would rephrase A as:

> **A. Loss of controlled mechanical geometry and closure force after the original roll-forming cycle.**

That's a stronger and more technically defensible formulation than “the aluminium shell bends and therefore can't reseal.”

# 4. Hypothesis B — liner compression set

This is the more interesting mechanism.

The sealing liner isn't simply “rubber stuck inside the cap.”

Depending on the closure, it can be polyethylene foam, an EPE-based sealing wad, or a thermoplastic elastomer compound such as SVELON.

For example, current BERICAP MCA28 ROPP products can use **SVELON** sealing systems and are offered in versions specifically intended for hot filling and pasteurization. ([Bericap][11])

ACTEGA describes SVELON as a TPE sealing compound developed specifically for crown corks and aluminium closures, with variants intended for hot-fill, pasteurization and sterilization. ([actega.com][12])

That's a very useful piece of evidence.

It tells us that:

> **“A liner” is not one material category.**

A closure with a purpose-designed TPE liner can behave very differently from a cheap EPE foam wad.

# 5. Compression set is real — and heat generally doesn't magically undo it

Under compression, polymeric sealing materials exhibit viscoelastic behavior.

Part of their deformation is recoverable.

Part of it is dissipative.

Over time, the residual sealing force declines.

This phenomenon is usually discussed as **compression stress relaxation** or **compression set**, depending on the test and material. The general behavior is well established for elastomeric sealing systems: residual sealing force is time- and temperature-dependent. ([PubMed][13])

The same basic problem appears in ordinary bottle closure engineering: after a closure is applied, the liner deforms to conform to the finish, and residual stress relaxes. Temperature accelerates the process. ([Industrial Physics][14])

For actual EPE foam, the situation is even more revealing.

Published EPE data commonly show measurable compression set. One commercial EPE grade, for example, reports 7% compression set at 25% compression and 15% at 50%; other grades report roughly 12–14% at 50% compression. ([H-O Products][15])

Research on polyethylene foam has also found that increasing temperature increases compression set and reduces recovery capability. ([Wiley Online Library][16])

That goes directly against the intuition:

> “Maybe soaking the old liner in boiling water will make it soft and let it spring back.”

Not necessarily.

Quite often you get:

**heat → increased molecular mobility → faster stress relaxation / creep → lower retained sealing force**

rather than:

**heat → magical reset to factory geometry.**

---

# 6. Your Hypothesis 1: can boiling water restore a compressed liner?

### Generally: no, not in a way that can be relied upon.

There are two different effects that are easy to confuse.

### Temporary thermal softening

Heating a polymer increases molecular mobility.

That can make the material temporarily more compliant.

When cooled, some of that deformation can be recovered.

### Permanent viscoelastic deformation

Some of the material's deformation has already been converted into a lower-energy configuration.

That part doesn't magically return to the original thickness.

With EPE, compression-set data demonstrate that some deformation remains after unloading and recovery. ([H-O Products][15])

And interestingly, PE-foam research finds **higher temperature → more compression set / poorer recovery**, not better recovery. ([Wiley Online Library][16])

So I would reject the idea that:

> “boiling the old cap regenerates the liner.”

It doesn't.

At best, heating could alter the instantaneous compliance of the material. At worst, particularly for an already aged/compressed foam, it can **accelerate further loss of sealing force**.

# 8. Why boiling creates the worst part of the problem during cooling

The cooling phase is almost as important as the heating phase.

Suppose you fill a bottle hot, cap it, and allow it to cool.

The contents contract somewhat.

More importantly, water vapor in the headspace condenses.

That can produce substantial sub-atmospheric pressure.

The resulting vacuum can actually **help pull the closure toward the bottle**.

This sounds beneficial — and it often is.

But it creates a second requirement:

> The closure must stay sufficiently compliant and sealed while that vacuum develops.

Commercial hot-fill lines explicitly treat closure integrity during cooling as a critical packaging variable. One published hot-fill closure test specifically evaluates whether cooling water can migrate past the closure, since successful prevention of ingress is an indicator that other external contaminants cannot readily enter either. ([Google Patents][17])

That's very relevant to your mold hypothesis.

The forum statement:

> “spoiled DIY pasteurized juice is almost always caused by reusing old caps that leak germs”

is **not scientifically defensible as a general statement**.

It takes one real mechanism and promotes it to *the* mechanism.

The National Center for Home Food Preservation identifies multiple causes of fruit-juice spoilage, including:

* inadequate processing,
* imperfect seal,
* air remaining in the jar,
* contamination associated with sealing surfaces,
* improper processing methods. ([Home Food Preservation][18])

For fruit juice specifically, the guidance says that an imperfect seal is one possible cause and recommends new sealing lids for each canning cycle. It does **not** claim that old lids are the universal explanation for spoilage. ([Home Food Preservation][18])

That's exactly the more scientifically defensible position.

# 10. There are actually two different contamination mechanisms

This distinction is important.

### Mechanism 1: contamination entering because the seal leaks

This is exactly what the forum explanation is referring to.

If the closure is imperfect, ambient air, microorganisms or contaminated cooling water can potentially enter.

That is a real and serious failure mechanism.

### Mechanism 2: the product was never adequately pasteurized

If the thermal process itself is insufficient, the closure could be absolutely perfect and the juice could still spoil.

The NCHFP explicitly lists inadequate processing separately from imperfect sealing. ([Home Food Preservation][18])

Therefore:

> **A perfect new cap cannot compensate for an inadequate thermal process.**

And:

> **A perfect thermal process cannot compensate for a closure that subsequently loses integrity.**

You need both.

# 11. Headspace is another overlooked factor

The home-canning literature also makes clear that headspace isn't cosmetic.

During processing, air and vapor are expelled and the contents contract during cooling. The resulting vacuum pulls the sealing surface firmly against the container. ([Home Food Preservation][19])

This means that the final seal is not simply determined by:

> “Did I screw the cap tight?”

It depends upon:

**cap geometry + liner compression + fill temperature + headspace + vapor condensation + cooling rate + bottle geometry + finish tolerances.**

That's why a system can look perfectly fine immediately after capping and then lose integrity hours later.

# 12. What happens during your “boiling water bath” scenario

Your proposed process is considerably harsher than ordinary room-temperature reclosure.

Consider the sequence:

**Cycle 1**

Factory cap → liner compressed → thermal exposure during commercial bottling → consumer opens cap.

Then:

**Cycle 2**

Used cap → washed → recapped by hand → bottle immersed in hot water → cap/liner heated again → bottle pressure changes → cooling produces vacuum.

You've now subjected the closure to a second thermal/mechanical cycle with:

* an already-conditioned liner,
* an already-formed aluminium shell,
* a broken tamper system,
* uncontrolled second application torque,
* potentially contaminated threads,
* potentially different headspace,
* and a different pressure history from the original commercial process.

That is an entirely different package.

# 13. Thermal failure isn't primarily an aluminium problem

This is worth emphasizing.

Aluminium itself is not remotely close to its melting point at 100 °C.

So “the metal cap can't handle boiling” is not a good explanation.

The more important thermal issues are:

### Liner modulus changes

The sealing compound becomes more compliant at elevated temperature.

### Stress relaxation / creep

Sealing force can decrease during prolonged heat exposure.

### Differential thermal expansion

Glass, aluminium and polymer liner all have different thermal expansion behavior.

### Geometrical recovery

The liner and shell don't necessarily return to exactly the same dimensions during cooling.

### Pressure reversal

During heating the internal pressure can push outward.

During cooling the package develops vacuum and pulls inward.

The seal must remain intact through both directions.

Packaging research on closure systems repeatedly identifies temperature, material relaxation and closure geometry as coupled influences on seal integrity. ([Packaging World][10])

# 14. Special case: one-piece HDPE cap with molded bore seal

# 15. But HDPE is not magically immune to creep

This is the counterargument.

Polyethylene and polypropylene are thermoplastics.

At elevated temperature they become significantly more compliant.

Generic HDPE grades can have Vicat softening temperatures only around the 110–120 °C range. One INEOS HDPE grade, for example, has a Vicat softening temperature of 116 °C and a heat-deflection temperature of 64 °C at a specified load. ([INEOS][20])

So at 100 °C:

**HDPE is definitely not “rigid plastic at room temperature, just hotter.”**

It is much more susceptible to creep.

PP generally has a higher thermal resistance range than HDPE, although the precise behavior depends strongly on grade. Commercial PP grades can have Vicat softening temperatures around 130–155 °C, but some grades are much lower. ([LyondellBasell][21])

That means material identification alone isn't enough.

A cap molded from an appropriately designed HDPE closure resin and geometry may perform excellently.

A random thin HDPE cap might creep badly.

# 16. Why molded-lip seals can nevertheless outperform old liners

A linerless seal is geometrically clever.

The sealing lip can be designed so that relatively modest compression produces a high local contact stress.

That means you don't necessarily need to squeeze a large slab of material.

Older linerless closure patents explicitly discuss this principle: shaped, highly compliant sealing sections are designed to accommodate dimensional variation while keeping the sealing material closer to its elastic regime, thereby reducing loss of sealing pressure during thermal processing and creep. ([Google Patents][22])

That's a major engineering advantage.

It's essentially:

**optimized spring-like geometry > generic compressed foam disk**

for repeated closure applications.

# 20. What is likely happening when an old cap “almost works”

Imagine an old ROPP cap that you clean and tighten.

At room temperature:

**Bottle neck**
→ liner compressed
→ enough contact pressure
→ appears airtight.

Then you heat it.

The liner softens.

Residual compression force decreases.

The aluminium expands.

The polymer expands differently.

Internal pressure changes.

Then the bottle cools.

The headspace pressure drops.

Now the package depends on the liner recovering enough to maintain intimate contact **everywhere around the circumference**.

A tiny local gap is enough to create a leak path.

You don't need a spectacular “cap popped loose” failure.

That is why visual inspection is such a poor proxy for closure integrity.

# 22. Your “hot fill” and your “boiling bottle” methods are not equivalent

This is another major point.

### Hot filling

The product is already hot when it enters the bottle.

The bottle is capped immediately.

The closure/neck are exposed to the hot product.

Some commercial processes briefly invert the bottle specifically to expose the closure and neck to the hot product before controlled cooling. Industrial hot-fill processes commonly operate in roughly the high-70s to low-90s °C range depending on product and process. ([Google Patents][17])

### In-bottle boiling

The sealed bottle is subsequently heated in a water bath.

Now the entire container/closure assembly undergoes an additional temperature cycle.

That produces a different pressure history.

And importantly:

**boiling outside the bottle does not guarantee that the juice inside the bottle reaches the same temperature instantly.**

The thermal process therefore depends on:

* bottle size,
* bottle shape,
* fill temperature,
* initial juice temperature,
* water-bath temperature,
* heat-transfer coefficient,
* pulp/solids,
* time at temperature.

# 25. The particularly dangerous misconception: “it held a vacuum once”

Suppose you reuse a cap and it passes this test:

> Fill bottle → cap → cool → cap doesn't rattle → bottle looks vacuum sealed.

That proves considerably less than people think.

You're testing **one state at one point in time**.

The second preservation cycle introduces a thermal history the first vacuum test did not reproduce.

A better engineering concept is:

> **seal integrity through the entire thermal cycle**

rather than:

> **vacuum present immediately after cooling.**

Industrial packaging engineering routinely treats closure integrity as a function of temperature, time, closure torque and material relaxation. ([Packaging World][10])

# 28. A useful way to think about closure failure

You can treat the seal approximately as needing:

$$
F_\text{seal} > F_\text{separation}
$$

where:

**Fseal** = residual force supplied by the cap/liner against the glass

and

**Fseparation** = forces trying to create a leak path.

Those forces include:

* internal pressure,
* vacuum-related deformation,
* thermal dimensional changes,
* surface irregularity,
* cap relaxation,
* liner compression loss.

Initially:

$$
F_\text{seal,0}
$$

may be comfortably high.

After thermal exposure:

$$
F_\text{seal}(t,T) < F_\text{seal,0}
$$

because the liner and/or closure relax.

The industrial engineer doesn't merely want:

$$
F_\text{seal} > F_\text{separation}
$$

at one instant.

They want:

$$
F_\text{seal}(t,T) > F_\text{separation}(t,T)
$$

**throughout the entire process and storage life.**

That is precisely why these systems require material/process qualification.

# 32. Final practical recommendation

For **ordinary short-term refrigeration**, reusing a good ROPP beverage cap can be mechanically reasonable because many ROPP systems are intentionally reclosable.

For **long-term home storage of pasteurized grape juice**, however:

### I would not rely on reused lined ROPP caps.

Not because aluminium suddenly becomes unsuitable, but because you're taking a closure with an unknown prior mechanical and thermal history and asking it to maintain a microbiological barrier through another thermal cycle.

### I would favor:

**new MCA28 closure + specified hot-fill/pasteurization liner**

or

**new purpose-designed HDPE linerless/bore-seal closure rated for the matching finish and temperature/process.**

Of those two, a properly designed linerless HDPE closure has an interesting theoretical advantage for repeated sealing because the sealing lip is an integral, repeatable geometry rather than a pre-compressed foam liner.

And one last important point:

### The closure should never be used as the substitute for a validated pasteurization process.

For grape juice, tested home-preservation procedures already demonstrate that **thermal processing and seal integrity are separate requirements**. ([Home Food Preservation][24])

So the most scientifically accurate version of the forum wisdom is:

> **Old caps can absolutely cause spoilage, but they are not “almost always” the cause. Spoilage is the result of an inadequate overall preservation system, in which thermal lethality, hygienic filling, headspace/vacuum formation and closure integrity all have to work together.**

That is, happily, much less mysterious than “the old cap leaked germs.” It is just thermodynamics, polymer rheology and microbiology conspiring in a bottle.
